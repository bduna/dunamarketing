Marden Estate Law

A five-page site for a San Diego estate planning practice, built so a prospect can make the decision on the first screen.

Open the sample

  1. Above the fold, the whole decision.

    The first screen shows everything a prospect needs to decide: the headline names the service and promises three meetings, one button places the call, the other opens the fees, and the three main prices are printed right under the buttons. Nobody has to scroll.

  2. Click-to-call, on every page.

    The header carries a call button with the number on all five pages, the hero button repeats it with “flat fees, printed” underneath, and every page closes with its own call-or-email section. On a phone each of those is a single tap.

  3. Every fee printed.

    The fee ledger sits on the home page and again on its own page: the will package for one person or a couple, the trust package, a plan review, and powers of attorney and healthcare directives included. It answers the question prospects most want answered and are least likely to ask.

  4. Six objections handled before the call.

    A FAQ on the home page opens with “Do I need a will or a trust?” and works through “What’s actually in the will package?”, “How long does it take?” and “I already have a will from years ago.” Each one is a reason not to call, answered.

  5. A process that lowers the stakes.

    Three meetings, usually inside three weeks, and nothing to sign until every page has been read. Prospects know what happens next before they commit.

  6. Local intent in every page title.

    San Diego is in the title of all five pages, in every meta description, and in the eyebrow above the headline. The site is built to be found by the person searching for a will in that city.

  7. Typography that does the persuading.

    Cormorant Garamond for the headline, with an italic second line for the promise, Inter for everything meant to be read fast, and the fees set in gold serif numerals. Deep green on warm paper, hairlines instead of boxes.

  8. Fast, accessible, and nothing to patch.

    Static pages, about two kilobytes of script, no plugins or CMS. A skip link, alt text on all sixteen images, an accessible menu button, and a unique title and description on every page.

Long Table Catering

A five-page site for a Sonoma caterer, built to turn a visit into a quote request.

Open the sample

  1. One conversion goal, repeated everywhere.

    The header button, the hero button, and the closing section of all five pages point at the same thing: a quote request that asks for the date, the headcount, and the kind of event. The visitor is never left wondering what to do next.

  2. A first step that costs a meal, not a contract.

    The process is three steps on the home page: send the date and the headcount, taste it before you sign, then they cook and you sit. The tasting is $75 and credited to the booking, so the first commitment a prospect makes is dinner.

  3. Starting prices, and what moves them.

    Weddings from $95 a head, corporate lunches from $28, family-style from $60, bar service by quote, printed on the home page and the menu page. The menu page then names the three things that move the number: headcount, service style, and the market that week.

  4. A point of view, not a list of services.

    The site explains why the food tastes the way it does: cooked on site over the kitchen’s own fire, from menus written around what is in season that week, with the parts that suffer from sitting finished last.

  5. Photography that sells the dinner.

    A full-bleed table shot in the hero, twenty-five photographs across the site and none used twice. The gallery captions them as real jobs: “These were all somebody’s date in a calendar.”

  6. Pages in the order people decide.

    What we do, then the menu and prices, then the gallery, then about, with a quote button in the header the whole way. That’s the order a person books catering in.

  7. A brand, not a theme.

    EB Garamond with true italics over Manrope, olive and terracotta on cream, an LT monogram in the header, and terracotta buttons that are the only loud thing on the page.

  8. Mobile-first, and it shows in the code.

    The stylesheet is written for phones first and widened for desktops, the menu collapses behind an accessible button, and every page carries a skip link and a canonical tag.

  9. Fast, and it doesn’t jump around.

    Every image carries its width and height so nothing shifts while the page loads, images below the first screen load lazily, and the fonts swap in without blanking the text.

  10. Nothing to update, nothing to break.

    Plain HTML and CSS with a few kilobytes of script: no plugins to update, no database, no login to protect.

Deep Roots Hydro

A Sebastopol grow shop, on its own domain, with no trace of me on it.

Visit deeproots707.com

  1. Built for a shop you walk into.

    Two buttons above the fold, Get directions and See what we stock, the phone number in the header, and eleven click-to-call links down the page. For a store the conversions are a visit and a call, and the page is built around both.

  2. Hours that know what time it is.

    The hero carries a live open-or-closed badge with the day’s hours, and the hours and address are also written into the page as structured data, so Google can show them next to the listing.

  3. The case for coming in.

    “A cart can’t look at your leaves”: the copy makes the argument for a real grow shop over ordering online, backs it with thirty years in the industry and twenty behind this counter, and closes with a seven-question FAQ taken from what people ask on the phone.

  4. The shop’s own shelves.

    The hero is a photograph of the actual inventory, and there are twenty images down the page, each sized so nothing shifts while it loads. No builder credit anywhere: the only name on the site is the shop’s.
