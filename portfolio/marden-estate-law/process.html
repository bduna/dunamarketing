How it works

Three meetings, about three weeks

Nothing to sign until you’ve read every page.

The kitchen-table conversation

An hour, at the office or at your table: what you own, who you trust, who gets what, and who’s in charge if you can’t be. You leave with nothing to sign and nothing to pay for yet.

The reading

The draft, in plain English, read together page by page. Anything that doesn’t sound like what you meant gets changed on the spot — that’s what the meeting is for.

The signing

Witnesses and notary arranged, every document executed properly, and you leave with bound originals and PDFs. Then it’s done, and it stays done until your life changes.

Why this is worth three weeks

The conversation that keeps getting put off.

Nobody wants to spend an evening deciding who raises their kids or who unplugs the machines. That’s why the first meeting is a conversation and not a form — the questions are easier to answer out loud, in order, with someone who has asked them a thousand times.

What you’re actually buying is the version of the worst week of your family’s life where the paperwork is the one thing that’s already handled.

“She translated everything. We signed understanding every page, which is not how I remember our first will.”

— R. & T. Okafor

The last ten minutes

The pen is the easy part.

By the signing, there are no surprises left — you’ve already read every page in the second meeting. California’s witnessing rules are handled for you, the notary is in the room, and the whole thing takes less time than parking in San Diego.

Meeting one is a phone call away
